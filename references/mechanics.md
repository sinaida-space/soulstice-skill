# How the questions are paced

Working notes on pacing, wording and when to stop. They are rules of thumb from coaching
and interview practice, not science, and nothing here is a claim about the brain. Do not
lecture the user with them in session: an explanation turns an inquiry into a talk and
stops it.

---

## Specific episodes beat self-descriptions

Asked “who are you as an artist”, most people repeat the answer they have given before.
That answer is rehearsed and nearly useless here.

- Ask for a specific episode instead (“the last time it went well: what time of day, what
  was in the room”). Detail of one moment carries what the summary left out.
- **This is why the concreteness ladder is the core technique.** Abstract questions get
  the story; concrete ones get the material the story was made from.
- Do not push into real trauma. Revisiting an old wound without care can hurt, and this
  instrument is not built to hold that.

---

## Recognition is cheap, correction is not

Picking a fitting description is far easier than writing one. That is the risk of choice
cards: someone can pick four plausible options in a row and never say anything of her own.

It is also the opportunity. Judging whether a specific sentence is true of you is easier
than producing one from nothing, and it tends to come back with a reason attached.

- The card is the prompt, and the question behind it is “what is off in this”. Follow
  every pick with a card that asks what it leaves out.
- Options that are each slightly off work better than options that include the right
  answer. A near-miss asks her to name the difference, and the difference is the material.
  Build layer 5 and layer 9 this way deliberately.
- Generic options fit everyone and so tell you nothing. This is why `references/cards.md`
  bans category options.
- Three fast picks with no “Other” text usually means the options are too easy to agree
  with. Make the next card more specific.

---

## Naming a feeling precisely helps

Putting a specific word to a feeling often makes it easier to work with.

- “What is the worry?” already does work. Layer 1 and layer 7 do much of theirs simply by
  getting things named precisely.
- Precision matters more than intensity. “Fear of being seen as decorative by people whose
  technical opinion I respect” does more than “fear of judgement”.
- Do not name it *for* her. A label you supply invites agreement. A card option is a
  supplied label, so the naming happens in the follow-up: she picks the near-miss, then
  says in her own words what it actually is.

---

## Surprise is what changes a view

A session that only confirms what she already believes changes nothing, however pleasant
it feels.

- A contradiction is useful. Hold both halves and do not tidy them.
- Kegan & Lahey’s assumption test works because it checks a belief in real life rather
  than in conversation. Always turn an insight into one small action she can try.
- If a session produces only agreement and recognition, it has not done its job. Ask the
  harder version of the question.

---

## The body often knows first

Many artists have a reliable physical “this is wrong” before they can say why.

- Layer 8 asks for that signal deliberately. “Where do you feel it when the work is right”
  is a real question with a real answer, and she can use the answer tomorrow.
- Reasons given for aesthetic decisions are often found afterwards. Trust the signal she
  reports over the theory she attaches to it, and say so if the two conflict.
- Strong recoil is as informative as strong pull. A thing she cannot look at says as much
  about her style as a thing she cannot stop looking at. Layer 5’s embarrassing-recurrence
  and theft cards and layer 1’s “what do you admire that you could never make” rely on this.

---

## Exposure feels like danger

Showing exposed work can feel as frightening as a physical risk. What matters in session
is the shame that comes with that fear.

- The fear in layer 1 is not a flaw and not proof that the work is a bad idea. It often
  rises as a piece gets closer to mattering; read that as a pointer to the real work.
- She may be ashamed of being afraid at all (“other artists just make the thing”). Say
  once, plainly, that the fear is common and aimed at the right target. Do not turn it
  into reassurance. State it and move to the next card.
- The refusal cards in layers 1 and 9 give the fear somewhere to sit: “I will not do X”
  turns a vague dread into a boundary she chose.

---

## Wanting is not enjoying

Wanting something and enjoying it come apart, especially around feeds, open-call cycles
and follower counts, where reward is unpredictable.

- Layer 4 exists because of this. An artist can want something intensely and get nothing
  from having it. The ten-year question (“would you still make it if nobody saw the result
  for ten years”) separates the two.
- Envy in layer 7 points at something she wants and has not claimed. It is reliable about
  the direction and unreliable about the target.

---

## Tired answers are worse

Self-reflection is tiring, and tired people fall back on their usual self-description.

- One question per card. Never batch two questions into one card, and never send two
  cards before reading the first answer.
- Cards are lighter than open questions, which is why the passage can run eleven layers.
  Spend the saved energy on the follow-up cards, not on more layers.
- The arc breaks are there for rest. Take them.
- A long passage is better across three sittings than one. Say this when offering the mode.
- Late answers are weaker, which is why Field, the most important layer, sits at
  position 5.

---

## Leave room after a hard question

Insight tends to arrive when the pressure is off.

- After a hard question, do not fill the space with another question or a summary.
- Recommend that the Compass be read once and left for a week before acting on it.
- The journal mode exists for this reason: spaced sessions work better than one long one
  for anything meant to change behaviour.

---

## Feeling judged shuts people down

An inquiry that feels like an exam produces a performance.

- Never grade an answer. No “good”, no “interesting”, no “that’s very honest”.
- Push on the sentence, never on the person. “That sounds like an artist statement” is
  about a sentence. “You’re being evasive” is about her and will cost you the session.
- If she gets defensive, go back to a concrete episode. Detail feels safer than
  self-assessment, and it goes deeper anyway.
